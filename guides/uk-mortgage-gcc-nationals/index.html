Guide for GCC nationals

Can a GCC national get a UK mortgage? Nationals from Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and UAE

Yes. Nationals of Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE can get a UK mortgage, whether you live in the Gulf, in the UK or elsewhere. You can buy a home to live in, invest in buy-to-let or buy a flat for a son or daughter at university, and Sharia-compliant home finance is available too. You will usually need a specialist lender, a deposit of at least 25% and clear evidence of your income and deposit. You do not need a UK visa to buy. Here is how lenders look at Gulf nationals and what to have ready.

The short answer

Being a Gulf national does not stop you getting a UK mortgage. What changes is who will lend to you and on what terms.

Most UK high street banks either decline applicants who live overseas or only help existing customers. For Gulf nationals living in the Gulf, the market is made up of specialist expat and foreign national lenders, the international arms of UK banks, some private banks and the providers of Sharia-compliant home finance. In every case lenders look at where you live, your income and your deposit as well as your nationality.

This guide covers nationals of Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates. If you are a British or other expat living in the Gulf, our guide to getting a UK mortgage while living in the Gulf is written for you.

Where you live makes a difference

  • Living in the Gulf. This is the most common case. A smaller group of lenders will consider you, and buy-to-let and buying for a child at university are often the easiest routes. Gulf currencies are widely accepted because most are pegged to the US dollar.
  • Living or studying in the UK. If you work in the UK on a visa, or you are a student, some lenders will look at your UK address history, UK bank account and UK income.
  • Living elsewhere. If you live outside the Gulf and the UK, lenders look at your country of residence and the currency you are paid in as well as your nationality, so the choice of lender can change.

How lenders treat Gulf nationals

UK lenders have separate criteria for foreign nationals, meaning anyone who is not a British or Irish citizen. These can include a larger deposit, a higher minimum income or a list of nationalities and countries of residence they accept. Some lenders limit the nationalities they will lend to, so the choice of lender depends on your nationality and where you live, not only on your income.

You do not need a UK visa to buy a property in the UK. For a home you will live in yourself, many lenders ask for UK residence or a visa that lets you live in the UK. Buy-to-let, or a home for a family member, is often possible without one. A flat in London used for family visits in the summer is a second home rather than a main home, and fewer lenders offer second home mortgages to buyers living abroad, so say so at the start.

Income in Gulf currencies

A UK mortgage is lent and repaid in pounds, so the lender converts your income into sterling and often reduces it, commonly by 10% to 25%, to allow for exchange rate movements. The UAE dirham, Saudi riyal, Qatari riyal, Bahraini dinar and Omani rial are pegged to the US dollar, and the Kuwaiti dinar is linked to a basket of currencies, which some lenders view favourably. Our guide to UK mortgages with Gulf currency income explains how lenders treat each currency.

Many Gulf nationals work for government, semi-government or large family companies, and salaries are often split into basic pay and allowances for housing, transport and education. Lenders treat allowances differently: some count them in full, some in part and some not at all. Business owners usually need two or three years of company accounts, and Gulf rental income can sometimes be counted with tenancy agreements.

Where income is paid in a foreign currency, changes in exchange rates may affect the amount you need to repay in sterling. For a mortgage on a home you or your family will live in, the lender must also have arrangements to limit that risk and must warn you if movements change what you owe or your payments by more than 20%.

Deposit and how much you can borrow

Most lenders for foreign nationals lend up to 75% of the property value, so plan for a deposit of at least 25%. Some ask for 30% or more for first-time buyers, self-employed applicants, new build flats or high value properties.

For a home you will live in, many lenders use around 4.5 times your income as a guide, after they have converted it into pounds and applied any currency reduction. They also look at your commitments, such as car finance, personal loans and any home finance in the Gulf, so the actual amount offered can be lower.

Lenders and solicitors must check where your deposit comes from. Keep the statements that show how it was earned or saved and how it reached the UK. Many lenders accept a gifted deposit from a close family member for a home you will live in, with a signed gift letter and evidence of the giver's funds. Fewer accept gifts for buy-to-let.

Credit history

UK lenders usually cannot see your credit record in the Gulf, so a strong record at home does not carry across automatically. Some lenders ask for a report from your country's credit bureau, and most run a UK credit search. If you have lived or studied in the UK, a UK bank account and address history help.

Buy-to-let, companies and buying for your children

Buy-to-let. For a buy-to-let mortgage, the loan is based mainly on the rent, which is paid in pounds. Lenders test that the rent covers the interest at a stress rate, usually by 145% for individuals and 125% for limited companies, so buy-to-let has more lenders available to buyers living in the Gulf than mortgages for your own home.

Limited companies. Some investors buy through a UK limited company set up only to hold property. This has different lenders, costs and tax treatment, and lenders usually want personal guarantees from the directors. A company based outside the UK brings extra UK rules and far fewer lenders.

Buying for a child at university. Many Gulf families buy a flat for a son or daughter studying in the UK, sometimes letting spare rooms to other students. Some lenders treat this as a home for a family member and others as buy-to-let, and fewer lenders offer it than ordinary buy-to-let. Whose name the property is in affects the lenders available, the stamp duty and what happens when your child finishes studying, so decide this before you apply.

Remortgaging. If you already own a UK property, you can remortgage to a new rate or release equity from the Gulf. Our guide to remortgaging a UK property while living abroad explains the options.

Sharia-compliant options

Many Gulf nationals prefer Sharia-compliant home finance. In the UK this is usually a Home Purchase Plan: the provider buys the property with you, you pay rent on the provider's share instead of interest and you buy that share over the term. Sharia mortgages are available for buying a home, remortgaging and buy-to-let, and are certified by an independent Sharia Supervisory Committee. Our guide to how Sharia mortgages work explains the details.

Tax when you own UK property

Most Gulf countries have no personal income tax, but UK property is taxed in the UK wherever you live. Rental income from a UK property is usually taxable in the UK, and landlords overseas normally register under the Non-Resident Landlord Scheme. Non-UK residents must report every sale of UK property to HMRC within 60 days of completion, even if no tax is due, and UK residential property can be within UK inheritance tax. How these rules apply depends on your circumstances, so speak to a tax adviser before you buy.

Stamp duty for GCC nationals

In England and Northern Ireland, buyers who are not UK resident pay a 2% surcharge on top of the standard Stamp Duty Land Tax rates. You are usually treated as non-UK resident if you were in the UK for fewer than 183 days in the 12 months before you buy. Residence is what counts, not nationality, and if you buy with your husband or wife and one of you is UK resident, you are both treated as UK resident.

Anyone who already owns a residential property anywhere in the world, and is not replacing their main home, also pays a further 5%. Owning a home in the Gulf counts, including a home in your family's name if you are one of the owners.

First-time buyer relief can still apply to non-UK residents, with the 2% surcharge added on top. It is only available if every buyer has never owned a home anywhere in the world, you intend to live in the property as your only or main home and the price is £500,000 or less. The documents you will need

A typical list is:

  • Passport, plus your UK visa or eVisa details if you live in the UK
  • National ID card, such as an Emirates ID, Saudi national ID, Qatari ID, Bahraini CPR card, Kuwaiti civil ID or Omani ID card
  • Proof of your address, such as a recent utility bill or bank statement
  • A salary certificate from your employer confirming your role, basic salary and allowances
  • Three to six months of payslips, if you receive them, and bank statements showing your salary paid in
  • For business owners, two or three years of company accounts and the trade licence or commercial registration
  • Proof of your deposit and its source, and a gift letter if your family is helping
  • Details of any UK property you own, and of your existing mortgage if you are remortgaging

Documents in Arabic usually need a certified English translation, and some lenders and solicitors ask for copies to be certified. Most of the process can be handled remotely, with electronic signing and video calls.

Before you apply

  • Decide what the property is for: a home to live in, buy-to-let, a home for your child or a second home for family visits. Each has different lenders.
  • Count your home in the Gulf. If you own one, include the 5% additional property surcharge in your budget.
  • Gather proof of income and deposit early, including how allowances are paid and how the deposit will reach the UK.
  • Decide whether you want Sharia-compliant finance before you apply, as the providers are different.
  • Avoid applying to several lenders yourself. Each application leaves a mark on your credit file. A Decision in Principle tells you where you stand first.

Questions from GCC nationals

Can a GCC national get a UK mortgage?

Yes. Specialist UK lenders accept nationals of Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE living in the Gulf, in the UK and elsewhere, for buying a home, buy-to-let and remortgages. Most high street banks do not lend to people living overseas.

Do I need a UK visa to buy property in the UK?

No. You do not need a visa to buy. For a home you will live in yourself, many lenders ask for UK residence or a visa that lets you live in the UK. Buy-to-let, or a home for a family member, is often possible without one.

How much deposit do Gulf nationals need?

Usually at least 25% of the purchase price, as most lenders for foreign nationals lend up to 75% of the value. Some ask for 30% or more for first-time buyers, business owners or certain property types.

Will UK lenders accept my salary in dirhams, riyals or dinars?

Many specialist lenders accept income in AED, SAR, QAR, BHD, KWD and OMR. They convert your gross income into pounds and often reduce it to allow for exchange rate movements. Housing and other allowances are treated differently by each lender.

Can I buy a flat in the UK for my child at university?

Yes, but how you buy matters. Fewer lenders offer mortgages for a home for a family member than ordinary buy-to-let, and whose name the property is in affects the lenders available and the stamp duty.

Are Sharia-compliant mortgages available to Gulf nationals?

Yes. Home Purchase Plans, where you pay rent on the provider's share instead of interest, are available for buying a home, remortgaging and buy-to-let, including for many buyers living in the Gulf.

Does owning a home in the Gulf affect my UK stamp duty?

Usually yes. The 5% additional property surcharge in England and Northern Ireland applies if you own a residential property anywhere in the world and are not replacing your main home. Non-UK residents also pay a 2% surcharge. Your solicitor confirms the final figure.

Do I pay UK tax on rent from a UK property if I live in the Gulf?

Usually yes. UK rental income is normally taxable in the UK wherever you live, and landlords overseas usually register under the Non-Resident Landlord Scheme. Speak to a tax adviser about your own position.
