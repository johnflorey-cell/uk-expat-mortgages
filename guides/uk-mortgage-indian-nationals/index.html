Guide for Indian nationals

UK Mortgages for Indian Nationals

Yes, Indian nationals can get a UK mortgage, whether you live in India, in the UK or elsewhere, such as the Gulf. You can buy a home to live in, invest in buy-to-let or buy a flat for a child at university. You will usually need a specialist lender rather than a high street bank and a deposit of at least 25%. Where you live and the currency you are paid in matter as much as your passport, and if you live in India, planning how your deposit leaves India is an important early step.

General information only. This guide explains how UK lenders usually approach Indian nationals buying property in the UK. It is not advice and not an offer of lending. Every lender has its own criteria, and they change often, so speak to a mortgage adviser before making any decision.

The short answer

Being an Indian national does not stop you getting a UK mortgage. What changes is who will lend to you and on what terms. Most UK high street banks either decline applicants who live overseas or only help existing customers. For Indian nationals living in India or the Gulf, the market is made up of specialist expat and foreign national lenders, the international arms of UK banks and some private banks. If you already live and work in the UK, many more lenders are open to you.

Where you live makes a difference

  • Living in India. A smaller group of lenders will consider you, and fewer accept income in rupees than in sterling or US dollars. Buy-to-let and buying for a child at university are often the easiest routes. You will also need to plan how your deposit leaves India, which is covered below.
  • Living in the UK. If you work in the UK on a visa, such as a Skilled Worker, Health and Care Worker, Global Talent or Senior or Specialist Worker visa, you can apply to many more lenders with UK salary and UK bank statements. With indefinite leave to remain, most lenders treat you much like a UK citizen.
  • Living in the Gulf or elsewhere. Many Indian nationals work in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait and Oman. Lenders then look at your country of residence and your Gulf salary rather than rupee income. Our guides to getting a UK mortgage while living in the Gulf, Gulf currency income and living in Dubai explain how that works, and there is a separate guide for Singapore and Malaysia.

How lenders treat Indian nationals

UK lenders have separate criteria for foreign nationals, meaning anyone who is not a British or Irish citizen. These can include a larger deposit, a higher minimum income or a list of nationalities and countries of residence they accept. Some lenders limit the nationalities they will lend to, so the choice of lender depends on your nationality and where you live, not only on your income.

For a home you will live in yourself, many lenders ask for a UK visa or other right to live in the UK. You do not need a UK visa to buy a property, so buy-to-let, or a home for a family member, is often more straightforward for a buyer living in India or the Gulf.

India does not allow dual citizenship, so if you have become a British citizen you will usually hold an Overseas Citizen of India card rather than an Indian passport, and UK lenders will treat you as a British national. Tell your adviser which passports and cards you hold at the start.

Income in rupees and other currencies

A UK mortgage is lent and repaid in pounds, so the lender converts your income into sterling and often reduces it, commonly by 10% to 25%, to allow for exchange rate movements. Fewer UK lenders accept income in Indian rupees than in sterling, US dollars or Gulf currencies, and those that do may apply a larger reduction. If part of your income is paid in another currency, or you have a UK or dollar salary, that can widen your options.

Lenders work from your gross income and will want to see it in your salary slips, bank statements and income tax returns. Bonuses, allowances and Indian rental income may count in full, in part or not at all.

Where income is paid in a foreign currency, changes in exchange rates may affect the amount you need to repay in sterling. For a mortgage on a home you or your family will live in, the lender must also have arrangements to limit that risk and must warn you if movements change what you owe or your payments by more than 20%.

Moving your deposit out of India

If you live in India, India's foreign exchange rules limit how much a resident individual can send abroad each financial year, and they also set rules on buying property overseas and on borrowing from abroad. Tax may also be collected at source on larger overseas transfers.

This often means a deposit cannot be sent to the UK in one go, so plan how it will reach the UK early, well before you make an offer. Check the current rules with your bank in India and a tax adviser, use official banking channels and keep every record. UK lenders and solicitors check the source of funds closely.

Deposit and how much you can borrow

Most lenders for foreign nationals lend up to 75% of the property value, so plan for a deposit of at least 25%. Some ask for 30% or more for buyers living in India, first-time buyers, self-employed applicants, new build flats or high value properties.

For a home you will live in, many lenders use around 4.5 times your income as a guide, after they have converted it into pounds and applied any currency reduction. They also look at your commitments, including rent, loans in India and car finance, so the actual amount offered can be lower.

Many lenders accept a gifted deposit from a close family member for a home you will live in, with a signed gift letter and proof of where the money came from. Fewer accept gifts for buy-to-let.

Credit history

UK lenders usually cannot see your Indian credit score, so a strong record in India does not carry across automatically. Some lenders ask for a credit report from one of the Indian credit bureaus, and most will run a UK credit search. If you have lived or studied in the UK, a UK bank account, a UK address history and a credit card used and cleared each month all help.

Buy-to-let and buying for your children

Buy-to-let. For a buy-to-let mortgage, the loan is based mainly on the rent, which is paid in pounds. Lenders test that the rent covers the interest at a stress rate, usually by 145% for individuals and 125% for limited companies, so buy-to-let has more lenders available to buyers living in India than mortgages for your own home. Some investors buy through a UK limited company, which has different lenders, costs and tax treatment.

Buying for a child at university. Many Indian parents buy a flat for a son or daughter studying in the UK. Some lenders treat this as a home for a family member and others as buy-to-let, and fewer lenders offer it than ordinary buy-to-let. Whose name the property is in affects the lenders available, the stamp duty and what happens when your child finishes studying, so decide this before you apply.

Remortgaging. If you already own a UK property, you can remortgage to a new rate or release equity from overseas. Our guide to remortgaging a UK property while living abroad explains the options.

Tax in the UK and India

Rental income from a UK property is usually taxable in the UK even if you live overseas, and landlords overseas normally register under the Non-Resident Landlord Scheme. Non-UK residents must also report every sale of UK property to HMRC within 60 days of completion, and UK residential property is within UK inheritance tax wherever the owner lives.

If you are tax resident in India, India may also tax your UK rental income and gains, and the UK and India have a double taxation agreement that affects how the two interact. Your residence status in each country decides what applies, so speak to a tax adviser who knows both UK and Indian tax before you buy.

Stamp duty for Indian nationals

In England and Northern Ireland, buyers who are not UK resident pay a 2% surcharge on top of the standard Stamp Duty Land Tax rates. You are usually treated as non-UK resident if you were in the UK for fewer than 183 days in the 12 months before you buy. Residence is what counts, not nationality, so an Indian national living in the UK may not pay it. If you buy with your husband, wife or civil partner and one of you is UK resident, you are both treated as UK resident.

Anyone who already owns a residential property anywhere in the world, and is not replacing their main home, also pays a further 5%. Owning a home or flat in India counts, including a share of a family property.

First-time buyer relief can still apply to non-UK residents, with the 2% surcharge added on top. It is only available if every buyer has never owned a home anywhere in the world, you intend to live in the property as your only or main home and the price is £500,000 or less. The documents you will need

Lenders and solicitors must check who you are, where you live and where your money comes from. A typical list is:

  • Indian passport, plus your UK visa or eVisa details if you live in the UK, or your residence permit if you live in the Gulf
  • Your PAN card and a second form of identity if the lender asks for one
  • Proof of your address, such as a recent utility bill, a lease or a bank statement
  • Employment contract or a letter from your employer confirming your role and salary
  • Three to six months of salary slips and bank statements showing your salary paid in
  • Your Form 16 and Indian income tax returns, usually for the last two years, if you earn in India
  • For the self-employed, at least two years of tax returns and accounts, often certified by your accountant
  • Proof of your deposit and its source, including the records of transfers out of India, and a gift letter if your family is helping
  • Details of any UK property you own, and of your existing mortgage if you are remortgaging

Most Indian documents are in English. Any that are not will need a certified English translation, and some lenders and solicitors ask for copies to be certified, for example by a notary.

Before you apply

  • Plan the deposit transfer first if you live in India, so the money is in place when you need it.
  • Decide whose name the property will be in, especially if you are buying for a child.
  • Count your home in India. If you own one, include the 5% additional property surcharge in your budget.
  • Leave room for the exchange rate. Borrowing at the very top of what you could afford today leaves little margin if the rupee or your salary currency weakens against the pound.
  • Take tax advice in both countries before you buy, not after.
  • Avoid applying to several lenders yourself. Each application leaves a mark on your credit file. A Decision in Principle from a lender that accepts your nationality, residence and currency tells you where you stand first.

Questions from Indian nationals

Can an Indian national get a UK mortgage?

Yes. Specialist UK lenders accept Indian nationals living in India, in the UK and in other countries such as the Gulf, for buying a home, buy-to-let and remortgages. Most high street banks do not lend to people living overseas, and some lenders limit the nationalities they accept.

Do I need a UK visa to get a UK mortgage?

Not to buy a property. For a home you will live in yourself, many lenders ask for a UK visa or other right to live in the UK. Buy-to-let, or a home for a family member, is often possible without one.

How much deposit do Indian nationals need?

Usually at least 25% of the purchase price, as most lenders for foreign nationals lend up to 75% of the value. Some ask for 30% or more for buyers living in India, first-time buyers or certain property types.

Will UK lenders accept income in Indian rupees?

Some specialist lenders do. They convert your gross income into pounds and often reduce it to allow for exchange rate movements. Fewer lenders accept rupees than sterling, US dollars or Gulf currencies, so income in one of those can widen your options.

Can I send my deposit from India to the UK in one go?

Not always. India's foreign exchange rules limit how much a resident individual can send abroad each financial year, and tax may be collected at source on larger overseas transfers. Plan the transfer early, use official banking channels and keep every record.

Does owning a home in India affect my UK stamp duty?

Usually yes. The 5% additional property surcharge in England and Northern Ireland applies if you own a residential property anywhere in the world and are not replacing your main home. Non-UK residents also pay a 2% surcharge. Your solicitor confirms the final figure.

I am an Indian national working in Dubai. Can I get a UK mortgage?

Yes. Lenders look at where you live and the currency you are paid in as well as your nationality, and many accept income in dirhams and other Gulf currencies. Our Gulf guides explain how that works.

Can I buy a flat in the UK for my child at university?

Yes, but how you buy matters. Fewer lenders offer mortgages for a home for a family member than ordinary buy-to-let, and whose name the property is in affects the lenders available and the stamp duty.
